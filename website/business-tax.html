BUS-C-003

What an S corporation return includes

  • Form 1120-S — the federal S corporation return.
  • Schedule K-1 for every shareholder, reporting each person's share of income, deductions, and credits.
  • Balance sheet and reconciliation schedules where your business is required to file them.
  • Shareholder basis tracking — often overlooked, and it determines whether your distributions are taxable.
  • One state return, where the state requires an entity filing.
  • A review step before anything is filed, and electronic filing once you approve.

Priced separately when needed

  • Additional state returns, where the business files in more than one state.
  • Bookkeeping cleanup, where the records need sorting before a return can be prepared.
  • The shareholders' personal returns — related work, separate returns.
  • Extensions, amended returns, and prior-year filings.
BUS-C-004

Deadlines worth knowing

Entity returns are due earlier than personal returns, and the penalty for filing late is charged per shareholder, per month. It adds up quickly, and it is entirely avoidable.

  • The S corporation return comes first. Your shareholders cannot finish their personal returns until they have their K-1s.
  • Late filing is penalised per shareholder. A return with three owners accrues three times the penalty of a single-owner return, for every month it is late.
  • An extension moves the filing date, not the payment date. Amounts owed are still due on the original deadline.
  • Start earlier than feels necessary. Entity returns surface questions — basis, distributions, reasonable compensation — that take time to answer properly.

Specific deadline dates change year to year and vary by state and fiscal year-end. We confirm the dates that apply to your business rather than publishing a general one here.

BUS-C-005

What to have ready

None of this is needed for an estimate — only to complete the return.

About the business

  • Last year's business tax return, if the business has filed before.
  • Profit and loss statement and balance sheet for the year.
  • Your S election confirmation, and the date it took effect.
  • Formation documents and the states where the business operates.

Money in and out

  • Bank and credit card statements, or a bookkeeping export.
  • Payroll reports, including officer compensation, if the business runs payroll.
  • Contractor payments and any 1099s issued.
  • Loan documents, and records of money you put in or took out.

About the shareholders

  • Each shareholder's name, ownership percentage, and any change during the year.
  • Distributions taken by each shareholder.
  • Prior-year basis figures, if you have them tracked.

BUS-C-009

Common questions

Do I need a separate return for my LLC?

It depends on how the LLC is taxed, not on the letters in its name. A single-member LLC usually reports on the owner's personal return. An LLC that elected S-corp treatment files an 1120-S. A multi-member LLC generally files a 1065. Tell us the situation and we will identify which applies.

Can you do my business and personal returns together?

Yes, and for most S-corp owners that is the sensible approach. They are separate returns with separate pricing, but doing them together means your K-1 flows into your personal return correctly and in the right order.

My bookkeeping is a mess. Is that a problem?

It is common, and it is workable. It affects the quote rather than whether we can help. We look at the records first and tell you what cleanup is needed before quoting.

Do you file partnership returns?

Conditionally. Form 1065 is offered only when a qualified preparer and a reviewer are both available for your situation. Ask and you will get a clear yes or no rather than being strung along.

Do you handle C corporations?

Not currently. Form 1120 is not part of our service today. If that is what you need, tell us and we will point you elsewhere.

What about payroll?

We do not run payroll or file payroll returns. We do use your payroll reports when preparing the S corporation return, so have them to hand.

What is reasonable compensation, and does it apply to me?

S corporation owners who work in the business are generally expected to pay themselves a reasonable salary before taking distributions. What counts as reasonable depends on your role, industry, and the business's finances — it is a judgement call, and one worth discussing with your preparer rather than guessing at.
